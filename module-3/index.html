What's the HOS?

HOS 3 (Hands-On Skill) is this module's main build and 10% of your course grade. It has four stages across the two weeks: Create / Scaffold and Evaluate in Week 5, then Analyze and Understand & Refine in Week 6. All four go in one GitHub repo, and you submit that repo's link once, in Brightspace, at the end of Week 6. The Muddiest Point, Concept Debate, Application Challenge and Knowledge Check are graded separately.

HOS 2 stops a bad change at the door. HOS 3 watches what happens after the door, because production changes even when you don't: customers order differently in summer, a help-centre article is rewritten, a model provider updates a model. Three kinds of signal cover the system:

  • The model: a drift report (Evidently) that compares this week's inputs with the training data, feature by feature.
  • The agent: a trace of every answer in Langfuse, each step with its latency, and an LLM-judge score for whether the reasoning reached the right answer for the right reasons.
  • Retrieval: a benchmark of the help-centre search: context relevance (Ragas) against latency and prompt tokens, for Chroma and Qdrant at three top-k settings.

Week 5 instruments all three. Week 6 is the harder half: checking the judge against your own grading, explaining one signal mechanically, and writing by hand the one alert you'd want to be woken for.

Anchoring analogy

A car's dashboard. The fuel gauge, the temperature needle and the warning lights don't drive the car; they tell you something is changing before the engine stops. A drift report is the temperature needle creeping up. A trace is the mechanic's diagnostic printout of one trip. A judge is a second driver rating each trip, useful only if you've checked that they rate trips the way you would. And the retrieval benchmark is the fuel-economy test: faster or more thorough always costs something.

Learning objectives

By the end of the two weeks you will be able to:

  1. Instrument an agent so every run is traced with per-step latency, and report p50 and p95. CO3
  2. Detect model drift by comparing current inputs with the training data, and choose a threshold. CO3
  3. Score an agent's reasoning accuracy with an LLM judge, and measure how far you can trust the judge. CO3
  4. Benchmark retrieval speed and context relevance across two vector databases and several settings. CO4
  5. Recommend a retrieval configuration for an agent, with the trade-off it accepts. CO4

Lecture: signals that warn before things break

≈ 60 min with videos3 short partsCO3 · CO4

Three short segments instead of one long one: watch the video, read the notes, then try the quick check before moving on. The notes cover everything you need, so the videos are a second explanation rather than a requirement. If something stays fuzzy, that's exactly what your Muddiest Point is for.

Part 1 — Drift: when the world moves ≈ 20 min

Watch first5:24 · IBM Technology

Watch for the difference between a model that's broken and a model whose world has moved. Drift is the second, and it never shows up as an error. Open on YouTube (human captions).

Go deeper (optional): Evidently AI, Machine Learning Monitoring: What Is Data Drift? (3:06, auto-generated captions only), from the makers of the tool you'll use.

Data drift means today's inputs don't look like the training data: a new delivery zone opens, a holiday falls on a different weekday, a thermometer is recalibrated. Concept drift means the right answer for the same input has changed: after a competitor opens, the same Tuesday gets fewer orders. A drift report compares distributions, this week against the training set, and flags the features that moved, with a statistic per feature (Evidently uses tests such as PSI or Wasserstein distance depending on the column).

Worked example

From week 5, temp_c reads 3 degrees high because a sensor was swapped. MAE barely moves at first, because most days aren't hot. The drift report flags temp_c in week 5; MAE catches up in week 8, when a heat wave arrives and the model under-staffs every zone. Drift is the earlier, noisier warning; accuracy is the later, certain one. You need both, and you need actual outcomes to compute accuracy at all, which is why drift matters when labels arrive late.

⚠ Common mistakes

  • Retraining on every drift flag. Drift in a feature the model barely uses may not matter. Check feature importance and accuracy before you retrain.
  • A threshold nobody chose. Defaults are a starting point. A threshold is a decision about how many false alarms you'll accept to catch real drift sooner.
Quick check

MAE looks fine, but one feature has drifted sharply. Should you retrain? What would you check first?

Part 2 — Traces, reasoning accuracy and latency ≈ 20 min

Watch first6:08 · IBM Technology

Watch for the judge's known biases (for example, favouring longer or more confident answers). Those are what your agreement check is looking for. Open on YouTube (human captions).

Go deeper (optional): Langfuse, 10 min Walkthrough of Langfuse (10:09, auto-generated captions only): traces, scores and prompt management in the tool you'll use.

Each run of pantry_helper becomes a trace: the user's question, every model call and tool call as a nested span with its inputs, outputs, tokens and duration. Langfuse receives them through ADK's OpenTelemetry instrumentation, so you add a few lines, not a logging call per step.

Reasoning accuracy asks whether the agent reached the right answer for the right reasons: did it look up the order before promising a refund, did it answer from the current policy? Nobody can grade thousands of traces a day, so a second model, the judge, scores each one against a rubric, and the score is attached to the trace. A judge is a model too, so you check it: grade a sample yourself and measure agreement. If you and the judge agree on 9 of 10, the number means something; on 6 of 10, it doesn't yet.

Latency is reported as p50 (the typical request) and p95 (the slow ones), and per span, so a slow retriever doesn't hide inside a slow total.

⚠ Common mistakes

  • Reporting the average latency. One 30-second request hides inside a 2-second average. Users remember the p95.
  • Trusting a judge you haven't checked. And re-check it when the judge's own model version changes: its scores can jump while the agent stays the same.
Quick check

The judge's average score jumps from 72% to 85% overnight, and nothing was deployed. Name two explanations, and how you'd tell them apart.

Part 3 — RAG performance: speed and relevance ≈ 20 min

Watch first8:11 · IBM Technology

Watch for how a vector database finds near neighbours quickly: an index trades a little accuracy for a lot of speed. That trade-off is what your benchmark measures. Open on YouTube (human captions).

Go deeper (optional): IBM Technology, What is Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)? (6:35): a refresher on the pattern from AI 410.

pantry_helper answers from 140 help-centre articles, chunked and embedded into a vector database. Retrieval has two numbers that trade against each other: latency, and context relevance (did the retrieved chunks contain what the answer needs?). Ragas scores relevance against a small set of questions with known good passages: context precision (how much of what came back was useful) and context recall (how much of what was needed came back).

The benchmark runs the same 30 questions against Chroma (embedded in the Python process) and Qdrant (its own server in Docker), at top-k 3, 5 and 10, and reports relevance, retrieval milliseconds and prompt tokens for each.

Worked example

Raising top-k from 3 to 10 lifts context recall from 0.71 to 0.86, adds about 15 ms of retrieval, and adds about 2,000 prompt tokens to every request, which costs more and adds more latency in the model call than the retrieval itself. Precision falls, because most of the extra chunks aren't needed. The benchmark has to report all of it, or "top-k 10 is better" looks free.

⚠ Common mistakes

  • Benchmarking retrieval with the model's final answer. A good answer can hide bad retrieval (the model knew it anyway). Score the retrieved chunks themselves.
  • Comparing databases at different settings. Same embeddings, same chunks, same top-k, or the comparison is about the settings, not the database.
Quick check

Users say answers got worse after the help centre was reorganised. The model and code didn't change. Which retrieval number would you look at first?

▶ Rehearse before the build: Open the Observability & RAG Benchmark Simulator: drift a feature, loosen the judge, and tune the index.

New words this week (6)
Data drift vs. concept drift
Inputs changing vs. the right answer for the same input changing.
Trace and span
The record of one run, and each nested step inside it, with inputs, outputs and duration.
LLM judge
A model that scores another model's output against a rubric.
Agreement
How often the judge's grade matches yours on a sample: the check on the judge.
p50 / p95 latency
The time within which half, or 95%, of requests finish.
Context precision / recall
How much of what retrieval returned was useful, and how much of what was needed it returned.

    Concept Debate

    Discussion board · 3 stages
    This week's question

    Is an LLM judge's score a trustworthy production metric for an agent's reasoning accuracy?

    You'll argue a side you're assigned, then argue the other one, then drop advocacy and synthesize. Nobody gets to post "I agree" — by Stage 3 everyone has argued both sides.

    How your side is assigned

    Before the first debate you join Debate Group 1 or Debate Group 2, either assigned by your instructor or chosen yourself, as long as the two groups stay close to even. You stay in that group all term; find yours under Communication → Groups. The sides rotate each HOS, so nobody argues the same kind of position all term:

    HOS 1HOS 2HOS 3HOS 4Integration (Wk 8)
    Group 1Side ASide BSide ASide BSide A
    Group 2Side BSide ASide BSide ASide B

    Not in a group? (for example, you added the course late) Before posting Stage 1, count the posts on each side and join the side with fewer; if they're tied, take Side A.

    Side A — Yes, with checksGroup 1 this HOS

    A judge checked against human grading is the only way to score every run.

    • People can't grade thousands of traces a day.
    • Agreement checks make the judge's error measurable.
    • A consistent, imperfect metric beats no metric.

    Side B — No, not on its ownGroup 2 this HOS

    Judge scores are another model's opinion; treat them as a hint.

    • Judges share blind spots with the model they grade.
    • A judge's leniency can shift when its own model version changes.
    • Business outcomes (refunds issued, repeat contacts) are the real ground truth.
    AI use

    You may use an AI assistant to test your thinking, for example by asking it for the best objection to your side. Your posts must be your own words and your own reasoning: don't paste or lightly edit AI text. If AI changed your thinking, say how in one line. AI Use Guide →
